**EU sunscreen filters** cover more of the ultraviolet A spectrum than anything sold in an American drugstore, and the gap has now lasted a full generation. European brands routinely use Tinosorb S, Tinosorb M, bemotrizinol, Uvinul A Plus and Mexoryl 400. American brands cannot. The reason is not chemistry, it is a regulatory pipeline the FDA has not moved since 1999. In 2026 the result is real. Americans buy sunscreen with weaker long-wave ultraviolet A defense than shoppers in Paris, Tokyo, Sydney or Toronto, and dermatologists treating pigment and photoaging on US soil work with a smaller toolkit than their European peers.

> **TL;DR**: The EU classifies sunscreens as cosmetics and evaluates filters through the [SCCS](https://health.ec.europa.eu/scientific-committees/scientific-committee-consumer-safety-sccs_en). The FDA classifies them as over-the-counter drugs and requires drug-grade safety data. Modern filters like **Tinosorb S**, **Tinosorb M**, **bemotrizinol**, **Uvinul A Plus** and **Mexoryl 400** are approved in Europe, Canada, Japan and Australia but not in the United States. They deliver higher UVA protection factors, better photostability and no white cast. The 2014 Sunscreen Innovation Act promised a faster pipeline; the FDA responded with GRASE data requests most manufacturers cannot meet. Americans in 2026 still choose from a filter list frozen in the 1990s.

## Why the EU and US regulate sunscreen differently

The EU treats sunscreens as cosmetics. The US treats them as drugs. That single categorical choice explains almost every difference downstream, including which molecules make it to shelves.

In Europe, Regulation 1223 of 2009 governs cosmetics. Any new ultraviolet filter goes through the [Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety](https://health.ec.europa.eu/scientific-committees/scientific-committee-consumer-safety-sccs_en), which evaluates the ingredient on human safety, endocrine effects and environmental impact, then publishes an opinion the Commission can adopt. Once approved, the filter is added to Annex VI and can be used at defined concentrations. The process is scientific, transparent and comparatively fast. Japan, Australia, Canada and much of Latin America run similar cosmetic frameworks and often mirror the EU list.

In the United States, sunscreens have been drugs since 1972, when the FDA moved photoprotection into its Over-the-Counter monograph system. Any active ingredient must be shown Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective, known as GRASE. The evidence bar is drug-level pharmacokinetics, systemic exposure, reproductive toxicity and carcinogenicity, evaluated by the [FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research](https://www.fda.gov/drugs). This bar exists for good reasons in other drug categories. Applied to a topical ingredient used seasonally, it created a bottleneck no manufacturer has broken since Ensulizole in 1999.

## The filters approved in the EU but not the US

Six filters explain most of the transatlantic performance gap in 2026. All are approved in the EU and blocked or unresolved in the US.

The first is **bemotrizinol**, also called BEMT or bis-ethylhexyloxyphenol methoxyphenyl triazine. It absorbs UVB and long UVA with high photostability, remains active after hours of sun exposure and improves the stability of avobenzone when combined. Approved in the EU since 2000. Still not GRASE in the US.

The second is **bisoctrizole**, sold as Tinosorb M. It is a hybrid particle that both absorbs and scatters ultraviolet across UVB and UVA, gives high UVA protection factors, boosts water resistance and is broadly considered one of the best modern filters. Approved in the EU since 2000. Blocked in the US.

The third is **Mexoryl SX**, or ecamsule, developed by L'Oreal in the 1980s. It covers UVA short and mid, is highly photostable, and after a Time and Extent Application process was approved in the US only for two specific L'Oreal formulas in 2006. It cannot be used freely by other American brands.

The fourth is **Mexoryl 400**, the newest filter to reach the market, launched in 2022. It targets the far end of UVA up to 400 nanometers, the region most linked to pigmentation and mitochondrial damage. A 2021 study in the [British Journal of Dermatology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34614192/) documented its coverage and skin benefit. Approved in the EU. Unavailable to American formulators.

The fifth is **Uvinul A Plus**, or diethylamino hydroxybenzoyl hexyl benzoate, DHHB. A broad UVA absorber with excellent photostability, widely used in modern European formulas. Approved in the EU. Blocked in the US.

The sixth is **iscotrizinol** and its relatives, high-performance UVB and short UVA filters used in luxury European and Asian sunscreens. Cleared in Europe. Not GRASE in the US.

## The filters Americans have to work with

The US monograph currently lists sixteen approved active ingredients. In practice only a handful appear in modern formulas.

Avobenzone is the only broad UVA filter available in the American drugstore, but it degrades in sunlight if not stabilized. Octocrylene stabilizes it and adds short UVA absorption but has come under scrutiny for benzophenone contamination on aged product. Homosalate covers UVB and short UVA at high concentrations and is one of the four filters the FDA has flagged for further safety data. Octinoxate covers UVB and is banned in Hawaii and several Pacific jurisdictions for reef concerns. Oxybenzone covers UVA short and UVB, is highly effective but is the most cited endocrine and coral concern in the American arsenal. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are the only inorganic filters and remain the two the FDA has already declared GRASE without asking for further data.

The lineup is workable. It is not competitive with what a formulator in Paris or Tokyo can specify. American brands compensate through smart combinations of avobenzone plus stabilizers plus zinc oxide, and some deliver excellent products. The ceiling on long UVA protection is still lower.

## What the 2014 Sunscreen Innovation Act promised

The [Sunscreen Innovation Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/113th-congress/senate-bill/2141) of 2014 was Congress reacting to the same complaint you are reading now. Eight ultraviolet filter applications had been sitting in the FDA Time and Extent Application backlog since 2002, one for as long as thirteen years. The law required the FDA to act on each within a defined timeline and set up a route for future submissions.

The FDA did act. It reviewed each pending application and, in 2015, issued responses on eight filters including bemotrizinol, bisoctrizole, Mexoryl SX, Uvinul A Plus, ensulizole in higher concentrations and others. Every one of them received a Not GRASE determination, with requests for extensive additional data on human pharmacokinetics, reproductive toxicity and dermal absorption. None have moved to approval in the decade since.

The reason is structural. The FDA is applying a drug-safety framework to molecules the rest of the world evaluates as cosmetics. Getting that data is expensive, and the market for a single sunscreen filter in a single country does not usually justify it. That is why the queue has not cleared even after Congress intervened.

## The GRASE data issue: why filters keep stalling

In 2019 the FDA proposed a rule that took the ambiguity out of what it wanted. Only two active ingredients, zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, were classified as GRASE. Two were classified as Not GRASE, PABA and trolamine salicylate, which had already fallen out of use. And twelve, including avobenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, octinoxate, oxybenzone, octisalate and Mexoryl SX at low concentration, were classified as data insufficient to determine GRASE status.

A 2019 clinical trial in [JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2733085) then found that four common chemical filters, applied at label doses under maximal-use conditions, were detected in blood plasma above the FDA safety threshold of 0.5 nanograms per milliliter. That study did not show harm. It showed absorption, which under the current framework triggers a demand for a full pharmacological data package.

The demand runs against how sunscreen is actually used. To generate the requested data manufacturers would need to fund non-clinical toxicology, carcinogenicity and reproductive studies for each individual filter, which for a cosmetic ingredient can cost tens of millions of dollars with no exclusivity payoff. In Europe the same absorption question is answered by the SCCS with margins of safety calculations that account for realistic use, and the record for finished sunscreen safety across decades of exposure supports that approach. The [Inserm dossier on ultraviolet radiation](https://www.inserm.fr/dossier/rayonnements-ultraviolets/) puts the balance clearly. The measurable harm from underprotection is greater than any documented harm from modern filters used as directed.

## What the gap means for real skin outcomes

For most Americans on light skin, the gap costs long UVA protection. That translates to more photoaging, more collagen loss and more sagging over decades, even when the SPF number on the bottle is high. The [AAD guidance on sunscreen selection](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/sunscreen-patients/sunscreen-faqs) tells consumers to look for broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, which is correct. The label does not tell them how much long UVA coverage they are actually getting, and in the US it is usually less than a European equivalent.

For melanin-rich skin, and for anyone dealing with melasma or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, the gap is more direct. Modern filters and iron oxide tints are the practical fix for pigment. American formulators can still build strong tinted sunscreens on the filters they have, but the ceiling on the ultraviolet piece of that protocol is lower.

For oily skin and heavy compliance concerns, texture is the driver of real world SPF. Modern EU filters make it easier to build ultra-light sunscreens people actually reapply. Our roundup of the [best sunscreen for oily skin](/blog/best-sunscreen-oily-skin-no-white-cast/) is heavily weighted toward European and Asian formulas for that reason. A 2023 review on this compliance question in the [European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology](https://eadv.org/) journal shows that application volume, not filter chemistry, is the strongest predictor of real protection. Both facts can be true at once.

## How American shoppers work around the filter gap

The practical workarounds are personal import for personal use, travel purchases and the small but growing catalog of American brands using zinc oxide at high concentrations and nano-particle titanium dioxide to build high UVA formulas with the tools available.

Personal import for personal use is legally tolerated in small quantities and is how many American dermatologists source the sunscreens they use themselves. Sites like La Roche-Posay Anthelios in the French formulation, Bioderma Photoderm, Avene Fluide Mineral, Isdin Fusion Water and Beauty of Joseon Relief Sun are widely bought online. The [ANSM](https://ansm.sante.fr/dossiers-thematiques/produits-cosmetiques) safety framework governing these formulas is the same one that has approved the modern filter list.

American formulators are also making the best of the current monograph. Coppertone, EltaMD, Supergoop and CeraVe now offer avobenzone-plus-octocrylene-plus-stabilizer combinations that push long UVA higher than earlier generations, and zinc-only formulas with cosmetic elegance that used to be impossible. The gap is narrowing on real product performance faster than it is closing on the ingredient list.

## What is likely to change by 2028

Two things could shift the picture in the next two years.

First, the FDA is under pressure from the American Academy of Dermatology, from patient groups and from members of Congress to reconsider its GRASE framework for topical filters. The AAD has publicly asked the agency to align its criteria with international norms for cosmetic ingredients. A rule change is possible but would require congressional appetite that has been intermittent.

Second, several major manufacturers are quietly running the exact pharmacokinetic and toxicology studies the FDA asked for in 2019, on individual modern filters. If any single filter package clears the bar, it opens a template others can follow. Industry insiders point to bemotrizinol as the most likely first modern approval, because it has the broadest EU safety record and the widest UVB-to-UVA coverage.

Neither path is certain. If neither closes, the transatlantic gap will still be open in 2028, and American shoppers will still be reading articles like this one.

## FAQ

**Why does the US have fewer sunscreen filters than Europe?**  
Because the FDA regulates sunscreens as over-the-counter drugs and requires drug-level safety data before approving any new active ingredient. Europe, Japan, Canada and Australia regulate sunscreens as cosmetics and use a proportionate safety framework. No new US sunscreen filter has been approved since 1999.

**Is European sunscreen actually better than American sunscreen?**  
For long ultraviolet A protection and cosmetic elegance, the top European formulas do outperform their American equivalents, because they can use modern filters like Tinosorb S, Tinosorb M, bemotrizinol and Mexoryl 400. For UVB protection at SPF 50, both markets deliver excellent products. The functional gap is in the UVA range most linked to pigmentation and photoaging.

**What is the Sunscreen Innovation Act and did it work?**  
The Sunscreen Innovation Act of 2014 required the FDA to make timely decisions on the eight modern filters sitting in its Time and Extent Application backlog. The FDA issued its determinations by 2015 and asked for extensive additional pharmacological data on each one. No filter has cleared the bar in the decade since, so in effect the law forced decisions rather than approvals.

**Is it legal to buy European sunscreen and use it in the United States?**  
Personal import of small quantities for personal use is tolerated and is how many American dermatologists source the sunscreens they use themselves. It is not legal for a US retailer to sell them or for a formulator to include the filters in a product marketed in the United States.

**Does the filter gap actually affect skin health?**  
Underprotection against long UVA is linked to photoaging, collagen loss and pigmentation in high-quality evidence. Whether the practical gap between a top American formula in 2026 and a top European one is clinically significant for a given user depends on skin phototype, use of tinted SPF and application volume, which the AAD emphasizes matters more than filter chemistry.

**Are the modern EU filters safe?**  
The SCCS, ANSM and Health Canada consider bemotrizinol, bisoctrizole, Uvinul A Plus, Mexoryl SX and Mexoryl 400 safe at the concentrations approved for cosmetic use. Long real-world exposure data across Europe and Japan supports that assessment. The FDA does not dispute the safety data; it asks for additional US-specific studies before granting GRASE status.

**Will the FDA approve modern sunscreen filters soon?**  
Not soon in a regulatory sense. Manufacturers are running the studies the FDA requested and industry watchers see bemotrizinol as the most likely first approval, but no filing has been cleared as of August 2026. A change in agency framework or a targeted congressional action could accelerate the path.
