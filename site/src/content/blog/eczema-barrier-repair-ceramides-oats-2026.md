Living with **atopic eczema** is less about finding a miracle cream and more about rebuilding a **skin barrier** that leaks water and lets irritants in. The good news is that the routine that actually works is short, cheap and evidence based, and most of it happens outside the shower rather than inside a serum bottle. This guide follows current dermatology guidance to explain what to use, what to skip, and when to escalate to a prescription.

> **TL;DR**: **Atopic eczema** is a barrier and immune disease driven by filaggrin loss and chronic inflammation. The daily foundation is a **ceramide-rich, fragrance-free moisturiser** applied at least twice a day and within three minutes of washing, paired with a **gentle syndet cleanser** and short lukewarm showers. **Colloidal oatmeal** is the strongest over-the-counter add-on for itch and redness. Flares need a **topical corticosteroid** or a **calcineurin inhibitor** prescribed by a clinician, then emollients hold the calm. Avoid fragrance, essential oils, hot water and harsh surfactants, they undo the routine faster than any active can rebuild it.

Atopic dermatitis affects roughly one in five children and one in ten adults in Europe, and the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/eczema) frames it as a lifelong barrier condition rather than a passing rash. Guidance from the [European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology](https://www.eadv.org/) and epidemiology work indexed by [Inserm](https://www.inserm.fr/) both emphasise that the routine matters more than any single product. Every ingredient discussed here can be checked on [SkinScore](/rankings) by INCI and concentration. If you also react to fragrance and preservatives, our guide to [fragrance as the hidden allergen in skincare](/blog/fragrance-skincare-hidden-allergen/) is a good companion to read.

## What atopic eczema really is under the skin

Atopic eczema is not simply dry skin. It is a chronic inflammatory disease in which the outer layer of the epidermis, the stratum corneum, is structurally weaker than it should be. Many people with eczema carry mutations in the filaggrin gene, which reduces natural moisturising factor and disrupts the lipid mortar that holds skin cells together. Water evaporates too quickly through this leaky barrier, irritants and allergens get in more easily, and the immune system reacts with the redness, itch and cracking that define a flare.

Two treatment ideas follow from this. First, anything that repairs the barrier reduces both the number and the severity of flares, which is why emollients are the backbone of every guideline. Second, when the immune system is already firing, only anti-inflammatory prescriptions can shut it down quickly, which is why steroids and calcineurin inhibitors are not optional in a real flare. Everything else in this article sits between those two pillars.

## Ceramides: the barrier ingredient that matters most

Ceramides are lipid molecules that make up nearly half of the stratum corneum by weight, and levels are consistently reduced in eczema-prone skin. Topical ceramides, ideally in a physiological ratio of ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids, restore the lipid matrix and reduce transepidermal water loss. Clinical trials on ceramide-dominant moisturisers show measurable improvement in eczema severity scores after four to eight weeks of twice-daily use, with a strong safety profile in adults, children and infants.

The practical takeaway is that a moisturiser containing ceramides 1, 3 and 6-II, or a broader ceramide complex, is the best default base for eczema-prone skin. Formulations that also include cholesterol and fatty acids are closer to the natural barrier composition and tend to perform slightly better in comparative trials. ## Colloidal oatmeal: the most studied OTC calmer

Colloidal oatmeal has FDA over-the-counter monograph status as a skin protectant and is one of the very few plant-derived ingredients with high-quality clinical evidence for eczema. It contains avenanthramides, which have measurable anti-inflammatory and anti-itch effects, and beta-glucans, which act as film-forming humectants. Randomised trials in mild to moderate atopic dermatitis show that colloidal oatmeal creams reduce itch, redness and dryness compared with baseline, and they are safe in infants from three months of age in most guidance.

A colloidal oatmeal cream is a strong companion to a ceramide moisturiser during a mild flare, either used together or alternated, and colloidal oatmeal bath oils are useful for widespread eczema on the body. Look for products where colloidal oatmeal appears high in the INCI list and where the formula is otherwise minimal, with no fragrance, no essential oils and few sensitisers. Oat proteins can, very rarely, trigger allergy, so patch test if you have never used oat products before.

## Humectants that pull water back into the skin

Alongside barrier lipids, eczema-prone skin needs humectants that draw and hold water in the stratum corneum. Glycerin is the workhorse, well tolerated, cheap and present in most medical-grade emollients. Urea at 5% to 10% is a strong humectant with mild keratolytic action that helps with the rough, thickened patches of chronic eczema, though higher concentrations can sting on active lesions. Panthenol, allantoin and hyaluronic acid all add hydration without meaningful risk of irritation.

The one caveat is that humectants alone are not enough. In dry indoor air, a humectant with no occlusive above it can pull water from deeper skin layers to the surface and worsen dryness. That is why the classic eczema formula pairs a humectant with an occlusive layer, either within the same cream or by applying an emollient balm over a hydrating lotion. Our guide to [common hyaluronic acid mistakes](/blog/hyaluronic-acid-mistakes-everyone-makes/) explains this dehydration trap in detail.

## Occlusives and the slugging question

Occlusives are the third leg of the emollient triangle. Petrolatum is the reference standard, it reduces transepidermal water loss by more than 98% and is a mainstay of eczema care in pediatric guidelines. Dimethicone offers a lighter, non-greasy alternative that many adults prefer. Squalane, shea butter and lanolin can also work, though lanolin is a documented sensitiser and is worth patch testing first.

The internet trend of applying a thick layer of petrolatum overnight, known as slugging, is essentially a home version of what dermatologists have been recommending for decades in eczema. On broken, cracked skin, use it only over a clean, moisturised base and only on unaffected or healed areas, since sealing a colonised lesion can worsen infection. Our long-form take in the [slugging with Vaseline article](/blog/slugging-vaseline-trend-dermatologist-opinion/) explains when it helps and when it hurts.

## The right way to wash eczema-prone skin

Cleansing is where most eczema routines quietly go wrong. Traditional soap bars raise skin pH toward alkaline, strip lipids and worsen barrier function. The evidence-backed alternative is a syndet, a synthetic detergent cleanser formulated at skin-friendly pH between 4.5 and 5.5, with mild non-ionic or amphoteric surfactants such as coco-glucoside or cocamidopropyl betaine. Fragrance-free syndets from pharmacy brands such as those covered in our [best cleanser for sensitive skin roundup](/blog/best-cleanser-sensitive-skin-gentle/) are inexpensive and well tolerated.

Water matters too. Short showers of five to ten minutes at lukewarm temperature protect the barrier, while long hot baths strip it. Pat the skin dry with a soft cotton towel rather than rubbing. Apply the emollient within three minutes of stepping out of the shower, while the skin is still slightly damp, because that is when it locks in the most water. This soak-and-seal step is one of the most cost-free, evidence-backed changes anyone with eczema can make. ## When and how to use prescription treatments

Emollients repair the barrier, but they do not shut down an active flare on their own. Once the skin is red, itchy and inflamed, guidance from the [Haute Autorite de Sante](https://www.has-sante.fr/) and the AAD is consistent: a topical corticosteroid of appropriate potency, used for a defined period, is the first-line anti-inflammatory. Mild steroids such as hydrocortisone 1% suit thin skin including the face, while moderate to potent steroids are used short term on the body under medical supervision. Applied correctly, using the fingertip unit rule, they are safe, effective and boring, exactly what a flare treatment should be.

Topical calcineurin inhibitors, tacrolimus and pimecrolimus, are steroid-sparing options approved by [ANSM](https://ansm.sante.fr/) and by the European Medicines Agency for atopic dermatitis, particularly on sensitive areas such as the face and skin folds. They can be used proactively twice a week on chronic sites to prevent recurrence, a strategy validated in multiple randomised trials. Newer topical Janus kinase inhibitors and biologic therapies for moderate to severe disease are dermatologist-prescribed and outside the scope of a self-care routine, but they exist and are worth asking about if your eczema is not responding to standard care.

## Triggers to avoid, ranked by how often they matter

The single biggest avoidable trigger in cosmetics is added fragrance, both synthetic perfume and essential oils. Fragrance mixes are among the top allergens on European patch test series, and eczema-prone skin reacts more strongly than average skin. If a product smells like anything, treat it as suspect and read the INCI. Our deep dive on [fragrance as a hidden allergen](/blog/fragrance-skincare-hidden-allergen/) lists the specific ingredients to watch.

Harsh surfactants such as sodium lauryl sulfate, high concentrations of ethanol, physical scrubs, hot water and rough fabrics such as new wool round out the practical hit list. Environmental triggers matter too, low humidity in winter, house dust mites, sweat, chlorinated pool water and stress can all set off a flare. You cannot control every trigger, but you can build a routine that tolerates a few of them without cracking. If you find that seemingly gentle products still sting, revisit the ingredient list against our guide to [comedogenic and irritating ingredients](/blog/comedogenic-ingredients-complete-list/) and simplify further.

## Building the daily routine step by step

A working eczema routine is short and repeatable, which is the point. In the morning, cleanse with a syndet or, on quiet days, with lukewarm water only. Apply a ceramide-rich moisturiser generously to the whole face and body. On days when you go outside, finish with a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or 50 designed for sensitive skin, avoiding fragrance and high alcohol content. For a comparison of pharmacy brands designed around this brief, our [Cerave vs Cetaphil breakdown](/blog/cerave-vs-cetaphil-which-moisturizer-actually-better/) and the [Cerave vs La Roche-Posay guide](/blog/cerave-vs-la-roche-posay-barrier-brands-2026/) both help pick a base.

In the evening, cleanse once with the same syndet, pat dry, then reapply the emollient within three minutes. During a flare, add the prescribed topical steroid or calcineurin inhibitor first, wait a few minutes, then layer the emollient over the top. On very dry patches, seal with a petrolatum or dimethicone occlusive overnight. Reapply the emollient any time skin feels tight, which for many people is three or four times a day, not two. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a special eczema cream or is any moisturiser fine?
Any fragrance-free, well-formulated emollient is better than none, but ceramide-dominant moisturisers, ideally with cholesterol and fatty acids, consistently outperform plain occlusives in eczema trials. What matters most is that the product is bland, fragrance-free, applied often enough and used generously. Pharmacy brands designed for atopic skin are usually a safer bet than boutique brands with long ingredient lists.

### Is colloidal oatmeal safe for babies with eczema?
Yes, colloidal oatmeal creams and bath oils are widely used from three months of age and are recommended in pediatric guidelines for mild to moderate atopic dermatitis. Oat allergy is rare but possible, so patch test a small area first if the child has no history with oat products. For infants under three months, ask a pediatrician or dermatologist before introducing any active ingredient.

### Are steroids dangerous if I have to use them regularly?
Used correctly, at the right potency, on the right body part and for a defined period, topical steroids are safe and are the fastest way to end a flare. The risks people worry about, skin thinning and rebound, are almost always the result of over-using potent steroids on thin skin such as the face for months without review. A clinician can prescribe the correct step-down plan and switch you to a calcineurin inhibitor for maintenance on sensitive areas.

### Can I use retinol or vitamin C if I have eczema?
During an active flare, no, both can irritate compromised skin. Between flares, on calm skin, some people tolerate a low-strength vitamin C serum in a bland base or a slow, cautious retinol introduction. Both are optional, not core, and if either causes redness or stinging, stop. Our [retinol for beginners guide](/blog/retinol-beginners-guide-how-to-start/) explains the slow-start approach that eczema-prone skin actually needs.

### Should I take any oral supplements for eczema?
The evidence for supplements is weak. Vitamin D in confirmed deficiency, and probiotics in specific pediatric contexts, have small supportive trials, but no supplement replaces daily emollient use and prescribed anti-inflammatories. Save your money for a large tub of ceramide moisturiser and a good syndet cleanser.
