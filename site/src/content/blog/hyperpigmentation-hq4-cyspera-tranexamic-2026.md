Choosing between **hydroquinone 4%**, **cysteamine**, **tranexamic acid** and **kojic acid** is the real question behind most stubborn hyperpigmentation, and the honest answer is that they are not interchangeable. Each one targets a different point in the pigment pathway, carries a different safety profile, and suits a different type of spot. This guide ranks them by the strength of the clinical evidence in 2026, not by which one a brand wants to sell you.

> **TL;DR**: **Hydroquinone 4%** is the dermatology reference standard for melasma, but it is prescription or pharmacist-controlled and meant for short, supervised courses. **Cysteamine 5%** (sold as **Cyspera**) is the strongest over-the-counter alternative and matches hydroquinone 4% in head-to-head trials. **Tranexamic acid** is the leading choice for hormonal melasma because it calms the signalling that drives it. **Kojic acid** is a gentler tyrosinase inhibitor best kept for maintenance. All four fail without daily broad-spectrum SPF, because UV and visible light switch the pigment back on faster than any cream can fade it.

Melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation are among the slowest skin concerns to shift, and the market is crowded with actives that promise a quick fix. Guidance from the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/melasma-treatment) and trials indexed on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) frame pigment treatment as a project measured in months, with sunscreen doing more of the work than most people expect. Every product referenced here is graded on [SkinScore](/rankings) by INCI, concentration and formulation quality. If you want the wider active landscape first, our guide to the [best serums for hyperpigmentation](/blog/best-serums-hyperpigmentation-dark-spots/) covers the gentler cosmetic tier before you escalate to the four heavyweights below.

## What these four actives actually do to pigment

All four treatments interfere with the same core process, but at different stages. Melanocytes, the pigment cells in the lower epidermis, convert the amino acid tyrosine into melanin using an enzyme called tyrosinase, then hand the finished pigment to surrounding skin cells. Slow the enzyme, quiet the signal that fires the melanocyte, or block the handover, and the spot fades over time.

**Hydroquinone** suppresses tyrosinase and is cytotoxic to melanocytes at higher concentrations, which is why it is powerful and why it needs supervision. **Cysteamine** is an antioxidant molecule that inhibits tyrosinase and chelates the copper the enzyme needs to function. **Tranexamic acid** works upstream, interrupting the plasmin pathway that tells melanocytes to overproduce pigment under UV and hormonal stress. **Kojic acid** is a fungal-derived tyrosinase inhibitor that acts more gently and more slowly than the others.

Because they hit different steps, the smart clinical question is never simply which is strongest. It is which mechanism matches the pigment in front of you, and which one your skin will tolerate long enough to see a result.

## Is hydroquinone 4% still the gold standard in 2026?

Hydroquinone 4% remains the reference against which every other depigmenting agent is measured, but in 2026 it is a supervised medicine, not a cosmetic you self-prescribe. In most of Europe, hydroquinone is banned from over-the-counter cosmetics and is available only on prescription or under pharmacist control, a position reflected in the cosmetic ingredient rules tracked by the [ANSM](https://ansm.sante.fr/). Dermatologists still reach for it first in difficult melasma, usually as part of a triple combination with a retinoid and a low-strength corticosteroid, for a defined course of a few months rather than indefinitely.

The reason for the caution is real. Long, unsupervised use of hydroquinone can cause exogenous ochronosis, a paradoxical blue-grey darkening that is far harder to treat than the original spots, along with irritation, dryness and rebound pigmentation if it is stopped abruptly. Used correctly, under a clinician, for a limited window, it is highly effective. Used as a permanent daily habit bought without oversight, it is a genuine risk. That distinction is the whole story: hydroquinone 4% is a short intervention, not a lifestyle active.

## How does cysteamine (Cyspera) compare with hydroquinone 4%?

Cysteamine 5%, sold most widely as Cyspera, is the closest over-the-counter rival to hydroquinone, and the head-to-head evidence puts them in the same tier. In a 2025 double-blind randomized controlled trial across three Indonesian centres, 5% cysteamine and 4% hydroquinone (each paired with ectoine) both reduced modified Melasma Area and Severity Index scores, with slightly greater improvement from cysteamine but no statistically significant difference between them, as reported in [Acta Dermatovenerologica APA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40127492/). In practice that means a non-prescription active performed on par with the prescription gold standard.

Cysteamine has a well-earned reputation for one thing: its smell. The molecule is sulphur-based, and early formulations were unpleasant enough to hurt adherence. Newer versions, including reformulated Cyspera, have improved this, and the standard protocol of a short leave-on contact time (often around 15 minutes before rinsing) keeps exposure manageable. Because it does not carry hydroquinone's ochronosis risk, cysteamine can be used for longer maintenance stretches, which matters in a relapsing condition like melasma. If your pigmentation is stubborn but you cannot or do not want to go the prescription route, cysteamine 5% is the most evidence-backed alternative available.

## Where does tranexamic acid fit, and is it better for melasma?

Tranexamic acid is the active to prioritise when pigmentation is hormonal, symmetrical and relapsing, which is the classic melasma pattern. Instead of only slowing tyrosinase, it interrupts the plasmin signalling that drives melanocytes to overproduce pigment under UV and hormonal triggers, so it treats the cause of melasma rather than only the surface stain. It comes in topical form at 2 to 5 percent, and, under specialist supervision, in oral and injectable forms for resistant cases.

The comparative data increasingly favours combinations built around tranexamic acid. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials covering 738 women, published in [Diseases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41149049/), found that intradermal platelet-rich plasma alone or combined with oral tranexamic acid ranked among the most effective options, outperforming hydroquinone 4% and several other single agents, with most adverse events mild. Topical tranexamic acid is gentler than hydroquinone, pairs cleanly with vitamin C and niacinamide, and rarely irritates. The old worry about layering niacinamide with acidic actives is a myth we unpack in the [niacinamide and vitamin C article](/blog/niacinamide-vitamin-c-together-myth-debunked/), so you can stack these brighteners without fear. For hormonal melasma, tranexamic acid is the modern lead active.

## What about kojic acid, and is it worth using?

Kojic acid is a legitimate tyrosinase inhibitor, but it belongs in the maintenance tier rather than the front line. It works more slowly and more mildly than hydroquinone or cysteamine, and it is prone to oxidising and to sensitising skin over time, which is why it is often blended with other brighteners rather than used alone at high strength.

The direct comparison is instructive. In a 2025 randomized study of 72 women with facial melasma published in [Cureus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40296942/), 5% cysteamine and 2% kojic acid both improved pigmentation over 16 weeks, with cysteamine showing a numerically greater mean reduction in modified MASI (12.25% versus 10%) though the difference was not statistically significant, and both were well tolerated. The takeaway is not that kojic acid fails, it is that its ceiling is lower. Kojic acid is a reasonable choice for mild, diffuse dullness, for holding results after a stronger course, or for people who cannot tolerate the heavier hitters. Treat it as a supporting player, not the star.

## The head-to-head verdict

Here is the honest hierarchy for 2026, matched to the type of pigment you are treating and to what your skin will tolerate over months.

| Active | Mechanism | Access | Best for | Key caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hydroquinone 4%** | Tyrosinase inhibition, melanocyte cytotoxicity | Prescription / pharmacist | Difficult melasma, short supervised courses | Ochronosis, rebound if misused |
| **Cysteamine 5% (Cyspera)** | Tyrosinase inhibition, copper chelation | Over the counter | Long-term melasma maintenance | Sulphur smell, contact-time protocol |
| **Tranexamic acid** | Plasmin pathway, melanocyte signalling | OTC topical / specialist oral | Hormonal, symmetrical melasma | Oral use needs medical screening |
| **Kojic acid** | Tyrosinase inhibition | Over the counter | Mild dullness, maintenance | Sensitising, oxidises easily |

A practical read of that table: if you have access to a dermatologist and your melasma is entrenched, hydroquinone 4% in a supervised course still sets the benchmark. If you want a self-directed but powerful option, cysteamine 5% is the closest thing to it without a prescription. If your pigmentation is clearly hormonal, put tranexamic acid at the centre. And use kojic acid to maintain gains or to treat mild cases, not to carry the whole job.

## Does your skin tone change which active to pick?

Yes, and this is where a lot of routines go wrong. In medium and deep skin tones (Fitzpatrick IV to VI), melanocytes are more reactive, so any irritation can trigger fresh post-inflammatory pigment that is worse than the spot you started with. That reality shifts the risk calculation for all four actives.

Hydroquinone is effective across skin tones but carries a higher relative risk in richer complexions, both because ochronosis is reported more often and because rebound and irritation feed the pigment cycle. That is one reason clinicians in skin of colour often favour gentler, longer courses built around cysteamine or azelaic acid, escalating to supervised hydroquinone only when needed. Cysteamine has a good tolerability record across phototypes, and tranexamic acid is well suited to darker skin because it rarely irritates while it calms the signalling that drives melasma.

The practical rule is to lead with tolerability in deeper skin tones and let strength come second. A milder active used consistently for four months, without flares, will out-perform a stronger active that inflames the skin every two weeks. Kojic acid and azelaic acid earn a place here precisely because they are gentle. Whatever you choose, introduce one active at a time, patch test, and treat any stinging or redness as a signal to slow down rather than to push through.

## Combination products versus single actives

Many of the most effective melasma protocols are not single serums but layered or blended systems, and the trials reflect that. The prescription reference is a triple combination of hydroquinone, a retinoid and a corticosteroid, used for a defined course. Over the counter, the winning approach is usually a stack: a tyrosinase inhibitor such as cysteamine or kojic acid, a signalling active such as tranexamic acid, a transfer blocker such as niacinamide, and daily sunscreen. The network meta-analysis data reinforces this, with combination modalities consistently ranking above single agents.

The caveat is that stacking multiplies both benefit and irritation risk. Add one active, give it two to four weeks, then layer the next, rather than assembling a five-step routine on day one. If a blended product already contains several brighteners, you rarely need to add more, you need to add sunscreen and time.

## How to build a routine around whichever active you pick

No depigmenting active works in isolation, and the supporting routine decides whether it succeeds. Three rules apply regardless of which of the four you choose.

**Sunscreen is the treatment, not the accessory.** UV and visible light reactivate melanocytes directly, so a broad-spectrum SPF 50 every morning, reapplied through the day, does more to hold results than any single serum. Tinted mineral formulas with iron oxides add protection against the visible light that worsens melasma specifically. If you have oily skin and hate white cast, our shortlist of [sunscreens with no white cast](/blog/best-sunscreen-oily-skin-no-white-cast/) applies directly.

**Layer for mechanism, not for maximum strength.** A tyrosinase inhibitor such as cysteamine or kojic acid pairs well with a signalling active like tranexamic acid and a transfer blocker like niacinamide, because they compound rather than overlap. Vitamin C both brightens and protects, and the forms and concentrations that actually work are covered in our [vitamin C serum guide](/blog/vitamin-c-serum-guide-forms-concentrations/). Retinoids speed cell turnover and help shed pigmented cells, but they irritate, so introduce them slowly as explained in our [retinol for beginners guide](/blog/retinol-beginners-guide-how-to-start/).

**Protect the barrier and go slow.** Over-exfoliation and aggressive stacking inflame the skin, and inflammation triggers more pigment, especially in medium and deep skin tones. A ceramide moisturiser keeps tolerance high, a point we detail in our guide to [ceramides and the skin barrier](/blog/ceramides-explained-why-your-barrier-needs-them/). Expect eight to sixteen weeks before you judge any of these actives, and if pigmentation is spreading, symmetrical, or not responding, see a dermatologist rather than escalating strength on your own.

## Safety notes you should not skip

These are active pharmacological molecules, and a few cautions matter. Hydroquinone should never be used long term without supervision, and should be stopped and reviewed if any grey-blue discolouration appears. Tranexamic acid in oral form is contraindicated in people with a history of clotting disorders, and requires medical screening before use. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, most of these actives are best paused in favour of gentler options and rigorous sun protection, as summarised in our [pregnancy-safe skincare guide](/blog/pregnancy-safe-skincare-ingredient-guide/) and in cosmetic guidance from the [Haute Autorite de Sante](https://www.has-sante.fr/). When pigment coexists with active acne or rosacea, azelaic acid is a gentle multitasker worth reading about in our [azelaic acid article](/blog/azelaic-acid-underrated-acne-rosacea/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cysteamine as good as hydroquinone 4%?
In head-to-head trials it comes very close. A 2025 double-blind randomized controlled trial found 5% cysteamine and 4% hydroquinone reduced melasma severity with no statistically significant difference between them. Cysteamine has the practical advantage of being available without a prescription and of not carrying hydroquinone's risk of exogenous ochronosis, which makes it better suited to the long, on-off maintenance that melasma demands.

### Can I buy hydroquinone 4% over the counter?
In most of Europe, no. Hydroquinone is banned from over-the-counter cosmetics and is available only on prescription or under pharmacist control, and it is intended for short, supervised courses rather than indefinite daily use. Products sold online that claim to contain cosmetic hydroquinone are a red flag, both for legality and for safety. If you want that level of strength, see a dermatologist.

### Which is best for hormonal melasma specifically?
Tranexamic acid is the modern lead for hormonal, symmetrical melasma because it targets the plasmin signalling that drives it rather than only slowing pigment production. It is usually combined with a tyrosinase inhibitor and, critically, with daily broad-spectrum and visible-light sunscreen. Recent network meta-analysis data ranks tranexamic acid combinations among the most effective melasma options.

### How long before I see results?
Plan for eight to sixteen weeks of consistent daily use before judging any of these actives, and longer for entrenched melasma. Pigmentation fades slowly because it sits in cells that turn over on their own schedule. Stopping sunscreen, or exfoliating too aggressively, will reset the clock by triggering fresh pigment, so patience and sun protection matter more than raw active strength.
